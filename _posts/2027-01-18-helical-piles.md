When a wall of a house has settled, or an existing wall is about to receive more load than its footing was built for, one common repair is to underpin the footing with helical piles. A contractor installs steel piles beside the footing, attaches each one to it with a bracket, and transfers the weight of the wall from the soil to the piles. I have prepared the calculations and the drawing sets for this kind of underpinning on existing house walls for a contractor more than once. This article is for owners and for the contractors who install the piles.

## What a helical pile is

A helical pile is a steel shaft, either a solid square bar or a round pipe, with one or more round steel plates welded to it near the bottom. Each plate is formed like one thread of a large screw. A hydraulic motor rotates the shaft, the helix plates pull it into the ground, and extension sections are added as it goes deeper.

The pile resists load mainly through bearing of the helix plates on the soil, much as a small footing does, and the slender shaft develops comparatively little friction. Because the plates bear in both directions, a helical pile resists a pull upward as well as a push downward, so the same product is also used to hold down structures against wind uplift.

## How underpinning works

An underpinning bracket is a steel seat that fits under the edge of the existing footing and is fastened to the pile at the side of the footing. The contractor excavates a small pit beside the footing at each pile location, prepares the bottom edge of the footing where the bracket will sit, installs the pile until it reaches the required depth and torque, and then sets the bracket and transfers load to the pile, usually with a hydraulic jack.

The engineering decides how many piles there are, where they go, and what load each one must support, starting from the weight of the roof, floors, and wall collected along its length. The spacing follows from the capacity of each pile and from what the existing footing can do between piles.

The bracket sits beside the footing, so the load reaches the pile at an offset from the center of the shaft. That offset produces bending in the bracket and in the top of the pile, and it tends to rotate the footing. The bracket, the upper pile section, and the footing are all checked for it.

The jack can also lift the wall, and owners sometimes ask for a settled wall to be raised back to level. Lifting has to be slow and done in small increments across all of the brackets together, because finishes that have adjusted to the settled position crack again when moved quickly. The goal can instead be to stop further settlement and leave the wall where it is.

## What the installation torque tells you

The torque needed to turn a helical pile increases as the soil becomes stronger. Load tests on many piles have shown that the ultimate capacity of a pile correlates with the torque measured during the final portion of its installation. The relationship is expressed as an empirical factor that depends mainly on the size and shape of the shaft, and the manufacturers and the testing literature publish values for common shaft sizes.

That correlation gives each helical pile a check during installation, which drilled shafts and spread footings do not have. Every pile produces a torque record as it is installed, so the installer and the engineer can confirm pile by pile that each one reached soil resistant enough to suggest the design capacity. A pile that falls short of the required torque at the planned depth is extended deeper or reported to the engineer.

The torque is still a correlation. It predicts capacity from the behavior of other piles in other soils, and the data behind the factor show scatter. A thin hard layer above softer material, or cobbles that resist rotation, can produce a high torque reading on a pile whose plates sit in a layer too thin to support them, with soft soil below. For that reason the design also relies on the soil information for the site, and on load testing of selected piles where the job or the soil uncertainty warrants it.

## Where the method reaches its limits

**Lateral capacity is modest.** The shaft is slender, so it offers little resistance to a sideways load in the upper soil, for the reasons described in the article [How a Pile Resists a Sideways Shove](/articles/laterally-loaded-piles/). In most underpinning products the bracket is designed for vertical load, and any significant sideways force on the foundation needs its own means of resistance.

**The existing footing has to span between brackets.** Once the piles support the wall, the footing acts as a beam spanning from one bracket to the next. Many older footings are unreinforced concrete, stone, or brick. If the footing cannot span the distance, the piles have to be closer together, or a new reinforced concrete beam has to be cast along the wall to receive the load.

**The design depends on reaching competent bearing.** The helix plates have to stop in soil that can support them, with enough depth of that soil below the plates. Plates that stop just above a soft layer push the load down into it. Very soft soil near the surface also provides little side support for the shaft, and a slender pile in compression can buckle in it. Steel in the ground also corrodes, and the design allows for that loss of thickness.

Underpinning is one answer to soil that cannot support the structure. The article [When the Dirt Is Not Good Enough](/articles/ground-improvement/) describes improving the soil itself, which is more often a choice for new construction than for an occupied house.
