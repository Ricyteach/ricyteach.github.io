A residential solar permit application usually needs a structural letter: a stamped letter report from a licensed engineer stating that the existing roof framing can support the array and that the attachments can resist the wind. This article sets out what the letter checks, the information that makes it possible, and what a letter based on photographs can establish.

## What the letter answers

The letter answers four structural questions about one roof: whether the existing rafters or trusses can support the added weight of the array, whether each attachment can resist the wind uplift on its share of the array, whether the attachment spacing accounts for the higher wind pressure near roof edges and corners, and how snow on and around the panels affects each of those.

The building code adopted by the jurisdiction sets the edition of ASCE 7, the loading standard published by the American Society of Civil Engineers, and the site address sets the wind speed, the ground snow load, and the exposure. The checks then combine those loads as described in the article [The Load Combination Is the Design](/articles/load-combinations/): a downward combination with the full weight of the array and the snow for the rafters, and an uplift combination with the weight reduced for the attachments.

## Added dead load on existing framing

Panels and racking are light, but the framing was designed before anyone planned to add them. Older houses often have rafters that span farther than current prescriptive span tables allow, rafters notched during later work, or several layers of shingles. A rafter is checked as a beam over its actual span, between the supports that exist in the attic, with the array weight added where the panels sit.

A truss is designed by its manufacturer as a system for specific loads, and its design drawings are rarely available years later. The evaluation works from the visible member sizes, web layout, spacing, and connector plate condition, and the letter states its assumptions.

## Wind uplift at each attachment

The array is fastened to the roof at points, usually a mount screwed through the roofing into a rafter or truss chord. Each attachment resists the wind uplift on its tributary area of the array.

Two capacities limit each attachment. One is the tested capacity of the mounting product, published by its manufacturer. The other is the withdrawal capacity of the screw in the wood, which depends on the screw diameter, the length of thread embedded in the framing, and the density of the wood species, calculated under the National Design Specification for Wood Construction or, for proprietary screws, from the manufacturer's evaluation report. A screw that misses the rafter and penetrates only the roof sheathing has a small fraction of the assumed capacity, so the letter requires the installer to locate the framing at each attachment. The rail manufacturer's span tables also limit the attachment spacing.

## Roof zones change the spacing

Wind pressure on a roof is highest near the edges and corners, where the flow separates from the roof, which is the same local peak effect described in the article [Wind on Structures That Are Mostly Air](/articles/wind-open-structures/). ASCE 7 divides the roof into zones and assigns higher pressures to the edge and corner zones. Recent editions include provisions specifically for panels mounted parallel to the roof, with conditions on the gap between the panels and the roof and with higher pressures for panels at an exposed edge of the array. Those provisions also include a reduction for pressure equalization, because air flowing into the gap behind the panels reduces the net pressure across them.

One attachment spacing does not work everywhere. Attachments in the middle of a roof plane can be farther apart than attachments near a corner, and a layout that uses the wider spacing everywhere is underdesigned at the corners. The letter or its calculations state the maximum spacing in each zone, and the installer follows the zone layout.

## Snow on and around the panels

Panels collect snow and pass that load to the rafters through the attachments. Snow also slides off panel glass and can pile at the eave or on a lower roof. The downward check on the rafters includes the snow load the code assigns to the roof along with the array weight, and the attachments are checked for the force along the slope of the roof as well as perpendicular to it.

## What to send

A complete request lets me write the letter without a round of questions:

- The site address, which sets the wind speed, the snow load, and the exposure.
- The rafter or truss size, measured, ideally with a tape across the member in a photograph.
- The spacing between rafters or trusses.
- The span, meaning the horizontal distance between supports, and what the supports are: an exterior wall, a ridge beam, an interior wall, or braces down to a wall below.
- The roof pitch and the roofing material, including the number of layers.
- The attachment product, its screws, and the racking and rail product.
- The array layout on each roof plane, with distances to the edges and the ridge, and the weight of the panels.

Photographs of the attic framing answer most questions that a description cannot. They show the actual member sizes, which in older houses are often larger than modern lumber of the same nominal size, the real spacing, braces, prior alterations, and any water staining, cracking, or sagging.

## What a letter from photographs cannot establish

A letter based on photographs is a statement about the framing shown in those photographs. I have not seen the roof in person, so the letter states its assumptions: the wood species and grade, the condition of members not shown, and that the photographs represent every roof plane that receives panels. Decay behind insulation and damage hidden by finishes do not appear in a photograph, and a vaulted ceiling with no attic access needs another way to confirm the framing, such as existing drawings or an opening in the ceiling.

The letter also directs the installer to stop and report conditions that differ from those described, such as framing at a different spacing, a decayed rafter, or a screw that cannot reach solid wood.

The letter covers the structural framing and the attachments. The condition of the roofing, the waterproofing of each penetration, the electrical design, and fire code access pathways are outside its scope.
