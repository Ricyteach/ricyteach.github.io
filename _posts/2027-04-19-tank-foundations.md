Water storage tanks, irrigation tanks, and process tanks look like simple foundation problems, since most of the load is the weight of the contents. That same load causes almost every difference between a tank foundation and a building foundation.

## The contents govern

Water weighs a little more than sixty two pounds per cubic foot, so a tank filled twenty feet deep applies about twelve hundred and fifty pounds per square foot over its entire floor, before the weight of the tank is added. A liquid heavier than water applies proportionally more.

That pressure is moderate compared with what an ordinary footing delivers, but it acts over a far larger area. A footing a few feet wide stresses the soil to a depth of a few feet. A tank forty feet across stresses the soil to a depth comparable to its diameter, and every compressible layer within that depth compresses under the load. A clay layer fifteen feet down, which a house footing would barely stress, can compress by inches under a full tank.

For large tanks on compressible soil, settlement usually controls the design, and bearing capacity, including bearing at the edge, is still checked. For the same reason, the soil borings for a large tank need to extend much deeper than borings for ordinary footings.

## Which settlement causes trouble

Uniform settlement is usually tolerable within limits, provided the piping is flexible enough to accommodate it at the tank.

The common forms of differential settlement are tilt, irregular settlement around the circumference, and dishing of the floor. Tilt of the whole tank is tolerable up to a limit. Irregular settlement bends the tank wall out of round and can damage the wall and the roof. Dishing, where the center settles more than the edge, is the natural result of a uniform load on uniform soil. Steel tank floors are often built with the center slightly higher than the edge to allow for this. The edge of the floor can also settle relative to the wall, which strains the weld where they meet.

The standards for steel tanks, published by the American Water Works Association for water storage and by the American Petroleum Institute for petroleum storage, include requirements for foundations, and tank suppliers set settlement limits for their products. The foundation design compares the predicted settlement with those limits.

## Ring wall or slab

A ring wall foundation is a concrete wall directly under the tank wall, with compacted granular fill inside the ring supporting the floor. It is common for large steel tanks with welded flat bottoms. The ring wall supports the tank wall and roof and confines the fill, and the floor and liquid bear directly on the fill.

A slab foundation is a reinforced concrete mat under the entire tank. It is common for smaller tanks and for tanks that need anchorage, since the slab provides weight and a place to embed anchors. A slab spreads the load and spans across small soft spots. It does little to reduce settlement of a deep compressible layer, because the pressure reaching that layer is about the same whatever sits on the surface above it.

When the soil cannot support the tank within the settlement limits, the options are deep foundations or the ground improvement methods, including preloading, described in the article [When the Dirt Is Not Good Enough](/articles/ground-improvement/). Large tanks on soft clay are sometimes filled slowly in stages during the water test after construction, with settlement measured at each stage.

## The edge where the tank wall bears

Even on a slab, the load is far from uniform. The tank wall delivers the weight of the wall, the roof, and any snow on the roof as a line load around the perimeter, and wind or earthquake overturning adds compression on one side. That line load sits near the edge of the slab. Near the middle of a slab, concrete surrounds a load on every side and spreads it in every direction. At the edge, concrete lies on only the inner side, so the slab can spread the load only inward, toward the center. Slabs for tanks therefore often have a thickened edge under the tank wall, reinforced for the bending and shear that line load produces.

Anchor bolts are also located at that edge, where concrete breakout near the edge often governs their capacity. Edge distance and embedment for those anchors are checked as described in [Why Anchors Fail, and Which Failure Decides the Design](/articles/concrete-anchorage-failure-modes/).

## Empty and full govern different checks

The full tank governs settlement, bearing pressure, and slab bending. It also governs earthquake design. During an earthquake, part of the liquid moves with the tank wall and part of it sloshes, and the overturning moment on a liquid storage tank comes mostly from the contents. The full condition therefore sets the earthquake anchor forces and the compression at the edge.

The empty tank governs wind. An empty tank weighs only its wall, roof, and floor, and a tall, light tank in a strong wind can slide or overturn. The contents cannot be relied on to resist that wind, because the tank may be empty or drained for maintenance on the day of the storm. Light irrigation tanks in open fields are a typical case, and I have consulted on wind requirements for irrigation tanks. A buried tank that is empty also has to resist flotation when groundwater is high.

Treating the contents as absent when they would help follows the minimum dead load logic described in [The Load Combination Is the Design](/articles/load-combinations/): a load that resists failure enters the check at its smallest credible value, which for tank contents is zero.

## Who designs what

The tank supplier designs the tank. The foundation is often designed by a separate engineer, who needs the supplier's reactions: the weight of the tank wall and roof per foot of perimeter, the operating weight, the base shear and overturning moment for wind and earthquake, and the anchor forces and layout if anchors are required. The anchor layout should be agreed before the slab is placed.
