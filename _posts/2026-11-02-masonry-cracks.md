Nearly every older house with brick or concrete block walls is cracked somewhere. An owner preparing to sell notices a crack and wonders what a buyer's inspector will say about it. A buyer reads an inspection report that mentions cracking and recommends a structural engineer. In both cases the useful question is which of the cracks mean something, and the answer comes from reading each crack as evidence of the movement that caused it.

Reading masonry cracks has real limits, and I am careful about them. A crack records that something moved. It does not always record why, when, or whether it is still moving, and some of those questions take more than one visit to answer.

## First, what is the brick doing

In most houses built in recent decades, the brick on the outside is a veneer: a single layer of brick standing in front of a wood-framed wall, separated from it by an air space and attached to it with metal ties. The veneer supports its own weight down to the foundation and keeps the weather out. The wood framing behind it supports the house. Cracked veneer is a real condition that deserves attention, but it is a different question from cracked structure.

In older houses, the brick wall may be several layers thick and may support the floors and the roof. There a crack runs through the structure itself. Establishing which kind of wall I am looking at is the first thing I do, from the wall thickness at a window or door opening, the pattern of the brick, and the framing visible from the basement or attic.

Concrete block basement and foundation walls are structure in either kind of house.

## Cracks that are usually not structural

Masonry materials change size over their lives. Concrete block shrinks as it dries after construction. Brick expands slightly as it absorbs moisture over the years. Both expand and contract with temperature every day and every season. Where a long wall is restrained from that movement, it cracks to relieve it.

These cracks tend to be narrow, roughly uniform in width along their length, and located where the wall changes: at the corners of window and door openings, at regular intervals along a long block wall, and near the corners of a long brick wall. They are common, and by themselves they rarely indicate a structural problem. They still let water in, and water is what turns a minor crack into a larger one.

## Patterns that point to movement

Some patterns indicate that part of the building has moved relative to another part.

**Stair-step cracks** follow the mortar joints diagonally up or down across a wall. A stair-step crack that runs from the corner of an opening, or slopes down toward a building corner, often indicates that one part of the foundation has settled relative to another. The direction of the slope suggests which part moved.

**Tapered cracks** are wider at one end than the other. A crack wider at the top than at the bottom, or the reverse, indicates rotation, which is usually associated with settlement or heave at one end of a wall segment.

**Cracks through the units** break the brick or block itself instead of following the mortar. Mortar is normally the weaker material, so a crack that cuts straight through a vertical line of brick suggests either a large force or a long, continuous restraint, and it deserves a closer look than a crack in mortar alone.

**Horizontal cracks in basement walls** are a separate case. In a block foundation wall, a horizontal crack near the middle of the wall height usually indicates bending under the soil pressure behind it, which I described in the article on bowing basement walls.

**Offset across a crack** is often more informative than width. If the masonry on one side of the crack has shifted in or out relative to the other side, or slid along the crack, the movement was larger than the opening alone suggests. I record both the width and the offset.

## Old movement or active movement

The question that most often decides what to do is whether the movement has stopped.

The evidence collected on site helps. Crack edges that are weathered and rounded, with dirt and debris collected inside, suggest an old crack. Sharp, clean edges suggest a recent one. Paint, caulk, or repointed mortar across a crack is especially useful: if the repair has cracked again, the wall moved after the repair was made. Doors and windows that bind in a way consistent with the crack pattern are further evidence.

When the evidence is not conclusive, the honest answer is that a single visit is a single observation. Crack gauges or measurement points fixed across the crack, and read over a season or through a wet spring, show whether it is continuing to open. Monitoring takes time, which can conflict with a sale schedule, so the letter states what the evidence does and does not establish.

## What a photograph cannot tell you

I ask for photographs first, because they often show whether a visit is needed. A photograph cannot show the depth of a crack, the condition of the footing below it, whether the brick is veneer or bearing wall, or whether the crack is moving. When the question depends on those, I say so instead of offering an opinion the photographs cannot support.

## The most common recommendation

The most common recommendation in reports on cracked masonry concerns water. Downspouts that discharge against the foundation, gutters that overflow, and grading that slopes toward the house all wet the soil under the footings, and wet soil, particularly clay, is where much foundation movement begins. Repointing open mortar joints and sealing cracks keeps water out of the wall itself. Structural repairs are sometimes necessary, but in my experience old houses with cracked masonry are frequently stable once the water is managed and the cracks are closed.
