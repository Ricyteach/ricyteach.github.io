Two kinds of engagement are often described with the same words. Both involve a second engineer reading a design prepared by someone else, and both end in a written report. They answer different questions for different clients, and an engagement scoped as one when the client needed the other produces a report that answers a question nobody asked.

## What a peer review asks

A peer review examines another engineer's design for technical adequacy. The client is usually the owner, the engineer of record, or a building department that requires one. The question is whether the structure as designed satisfies the governing code and whether the analysis represents the structure on the drawings.

The work is the sequence I described in [What I Look For When I Review Somebody Else's Design](/articles/how-peer-review-works/): the load derivation first, then whether the model matches the structure, then which limit states apply and whether each one was checked, then whether the drawings agree with the calculations. For the critical results, I run independent calculations along the load paths that produce them and compare those calculations with the submitted values.

The deliverable is a review report listing comments, most of them questions, followed by a round of responses from the engineer of record and a closing letter once each comment is resolved. Peer review is an exchange between engineers, and the engineer of record keeps that role throughout. The reviewer does not take over the design or the professional responsibility for it.

## What an independent engineering review asks

An independent engineering review is commissioned by a lender, an investor, or sometimes an insurer, before money is committed to a project. It is common in solar projects and in other projects financed by loans that are repaid from the revenue the project produces. The party commissioning it is deciding whether to finance the project, and the question it wants answered is whether the project as designed presents acceptable technical risk.

That question is broader than code compliance and less detailed in some places. The reviewer looks at whether the design basis fits the site: whether the wind speed, snow load, and seismic parameters are correct for the location, whether the geotechnical investigation supports the foundation design, and whether field testing, such as pile load tests, confirms the assumed capacities. The reviewer looks at whether the manufacturer's calculations cover the configuration actually being built, since a racking manufacturer's standard calculations often cover a range of tilt angles, spans, and heights, and a project can fall outside that range. The reviewer looks for conditions that could cause failures or performance problems over the life of the project, such as corrosion of steel embedded in aggressive soil, frost heave of shallow posts, or a foundation that relies on soil properties nobody measured.

The deliverable is a report addressed to the financing party. It documents what was reviewed, the findings, the items requiring resolution before financing or construction proceeds, and a professional opinion on the system as designed. It is written for a reader who may not be an engineer and who needs to understand which findings affect the investment.

A structural independent engineering review is usually one part of a larger review. In a solar project, other specialists address the electrical design, the energy production estimate, the equipment, the contracts, and the budget. My part is the racking structure and its foundations, which is the scope offered on my [solar racking page](/solar-racking/).

## Independence

Both engagements require that the reviewer had no part in the design. The requirements beyond that differ.

A peer reviewer may be hired by the engineer of record, and that arrangement is common and acceptable. Where a building department requires the review, it may set who retains the reviewer. The reviewer's obligation is to the technical question, and the review is collaborative in the sense that the goal is a correct design both engineers agree on.

An independent engineer works for the financing party and reports to it. The independent engineer has no financial interest in the project going forward, no business relationship with the developer that would compromise the opinion, and no role in the design or construction. Lenders often require a written statement of independence as a condition of the engagement. The developer pays for the review in many cases, but the report is addressed to the lender, and the lender relies on it.

An owner's engineer is a third role that appears in the same projects. An owner's engineer works for the owner, reviews the design and construction on the owner's behalf, and advocates for the owner's interests. That is a legitimate engagement. It is an advocacy role, so it does not meet the independence required of an independent engineer. An engineer can fill only one of the two roles on a given project, and the engagement letter should state which.

## What each one leaves out

A peer review does not establish that the structure will be built as designed. Construction observation, testing, and inspection are separate services. A peer review also does not address cost, schedule, or whether the design is economical, unless the scope says so.

An independent engineering review does not usually check every member and connection. It concentrates on the items that present risk, and it samples the calculations to confirm that the method is sound and the inputs are correct. It does not approve the design for permit, which is the building department's role, and it does not make the reviewer responsible for the design, which stays with the engineer of record.

Both engagements depend on a complete [calculation package](/articles/calculation-package/). A reviewer can only confirm what the package shows, and a package that omits the load derivation or the model figures forces either a request for more information or an opinion with stated exceptions.

## Setting the scope

The most useful step at the start of either engagement is a written statement of the question being answered and who will rely on the answer. That statement decides the depth of the review, the form of the report, and the independence the reviewer has to demonstrate.
