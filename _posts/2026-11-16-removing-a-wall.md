The question homeowners and contractors ask me most often is whether a wall can come out, usually to open a kitchen to the rooms beside it. In most cases it can, because a wall that supports load can be replaced by a beam and posts. The useful questions are what the beam has to support, where it delivers that load, and what has to happen below it.

## Is the wall load bearing

Whether a wall is load bearing depends on what rests on top of it and what is underneath it. Knocking on it, measuring its thickness, and judging how solid it feels do not answer the question.

Above the wall, the question is whether floor joists, ceiling joists, or rafters bear on its top plate. Joists that run perpendicular to the wall and end on it, or lap over it, are supported by it. In the attic, ceiling joists that splice or overlap above a wall are a strong indication. A wall directly above it on the next floor usually means the upper wall is delivering load to it.

Below the wall, the question is what supports it. A beam or a line of posts in the basement directly under the wall, or a wall in the basement or crawlspace in the same position, suggests the wall is part of a load path down to the foundation. A wall near the middle of a house, running parallel to the ridge, is frequently bearing for exactly this reason.

There are exceptions in both directions. A wall can be bearing even where the joists run parallel to it, because a beam or a post elsewhere may be delivering a concentrated load to it. A wall can sit directly under joists without supporting them, because the joists span past it. Older houses that have been remodeled before often contain framing that does not match any of the usual patterns. Opening the ceiling or looking from the attic and the basement settles questions that guessing cannot.

## Where the load goes

A bearing wall collects load along its length and delivers it continuously to whatever is below. Replacing it with a beam changes that. The beam collects the same load and delivers all of it at its two ends, through posts, as concentrated loads.

Those concentrated loads have to reach the ground. That means the post must bear on something capable of supporting it, all the way down: a post or a solid block of framing within the floor below, then a beam, wall, or footing in the basement or crawlspace. A post that ends on a subfloor, or on a basement floor slab of ordinary thickness, is a common error. A floor slab is generally not designed as a footing, and a new footing below the post is frequently part of the work. This is the reason a wall removal sometimes becomes a foundation question.

## Choosing the beam

The beam can be built up from dimensional lumber, made of engineered lumber such as laminated veneer lumber, or made of steel. The choice depends on the span, the load, the depth available, and how the beam will be installed.

A dropped beam hangs below the ceiling with the joists resting on top of it. It is simpler to build and to support temporarily, and it leaves a visible beam across the opening. A flush beam sits within the depth of the ceiling framing, with the joists hung from its sides on metal hangers. It leaves a flat ceiling, it needs a deeper or stronger member for the same span, and the hangers and their fasteners become part of the design.

## Deflection usually governs

For a beam in a house, the controlling requirement is often stiffness. A beam can be strong enough and still sag enough to crack a plaster ceiling, crack tile on the floor above, or make doors in the wall above bind. Building codes limit deflection as a fraction of the span, with tighter limits where brittle finishes are present, and I select the beam to meet those limits. Engineered lumber and steel are often chosen because they are stiffer for a given depth.

## Temporary support during the work

Before the wall is removed, the structure above has to be supported temporarily, usually with shoring walls or posts on one or both sides of the wall, bearing on something that can accept the load. The sequence and the shoring are the contractor's work, but I describe what the temporary support has to do when the loads are significant or the floor below is questionable, because a wall removal that goes wrong usually goes wrong during construction.

## What else complicates it

Plumbing vents, drain lines, ductwork, and wiring often run inside the wall and have to be rerouted. A wall below a roof with no structural ridge beam may be resisting more than vertical load. A wall in a house with more than one floor above it may be supporting several levels of load. Prior remodeling may have already removed something that was doing work. None of these prevent the project, but each one changes it.

## What the building department wants

Most building departments require a permit for removing a bearing wall, and many ask for the beam, post, and footing to be designed by a licensed engineer or justified by a published span table. What I provide depends on the jurisdiction and the contractor: typically a stamped letter or a sketch with the beam size and material, the post size, the connections, and the footing, which is what the inspector checks against and what the contractor builds from.

## What to send me

Photographs of the wall from both sides, of the framing above it from the attic or an opening in the ceiling, and of whatever is below it in the basement or crawlspace. The length of the opening you want, the direction the joists run, the number of floors above, and the approximate age of the house. With that, I can usually tell you whether the wall is bearing and what the scope of the engineering will be.
