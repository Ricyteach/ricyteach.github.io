If you have ever called an engineering firm with a small problem and been politely declined, or quoted a number that made no sense for the size of the job, you have run into a real structural feature of the industry, not bad luck. Most established firms are built around larger projects. A two-hour job carries the same overhead as a two-month one in their world, so the small stuff does not pencil, and they let it go. I built my practice to catch exactly what they drop.

## What "small" looks like

The work I am talking about is the genuinely bounded task that still needs a licensed engineer. A stamped letter confirming a deck or a re-roof is adequate. A single connection check. A quick look at one beam in a remodel. A foundation for one sign. A deflection check on a single run of pipe. Plan review and a seal on a package someone else drew. These are real engineering, they carry real responsibility, and they are too small to interest a firm with a business-development department and a project-management layer. For a lean solo practice, a small job is just a small job, not a problem to be avoided.

## How an engagement starts, and roughly what it costs

I would rather be direct about this so you can decide before you even email. Engagements start with a short consultation at no charge, where we figure out whether what you need is actually a small job or a larger one wearing a small disguise. If it is small and well defined, it gets a scoped, fixed proposal. There is a practical floor, since even a quick stamped opinion involves real review and real liability, so the very smallest jobs land in a modest minimum rather than being free or trivially cheap. But that floor is low enough that most people with a genuine one-off question find it entirely reasonable, and a two-line email is usually enough for me to tell you fast whether I am the right fit and what it will roughly run.

## Why I can do it when others cannot

Two reasons. The first is overhead: a one-person practice does not have to feed an organization, so the economics of a small job actually work. The second is that I have spent real effort building tools that make well-defined, repeatable work fast and reliable, which means the time a small job takes me is genuinely small, and I can price it accordingly without cutting corners on the engineering or the documentation.

## Who this is for

If three larger firms have already told you no, or quoted you as though your addition were a parking garage, this is the message I want you to hear: bring me the small one. The deck letter, the single culvert, the one connection that is keeping a permit from being issued. It is not a nuisance to me. It is the work I deliberately set up to do, and I will give it the same care I give anything else, because my name goes on it either way.
