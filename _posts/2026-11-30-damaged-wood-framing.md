A landlord finds soft wood in a crawlspace. An investor's contractor opens a wall to replace a window and finds the bottom of the framing has turned to fiber. A city inspector writes that a structural engineer must evaluate a damaged bearing wall before a repair permit will be issued. Deteriorated wood framing is a common reason owners in the Dayton, Springfield, and Columbus areas call me to an older building, and the questions are nearly always the same: how much is damaged, what caused it, and what has to be replaced.

## Where the damage concentrates

Wood decays when it stays wet for a long time. Dry wood does not decay, and wood that gets wet occasionally and dries quickly rarely does. As a result, the damage concentrates where water collects and stays.

The usual locations are predictable. Sill plates and rim joists sit on top of the foundation, close to the ground, where rain splashing off the soil and water running down the outside of the wall reach them first. The ends of floor joists bear on those sills. The bottom plates and the lower ends of studs in exterior walls sit below windows and doors, which often leak. Framing around bathrooms and kitchens is exposed to plumbing leaks that may have continued for years before anyone noticed. Additions and small projections from the wall such as bay windows, where a new roof or wall meets the original building, often have flashing details that never worked.

Damage along one wall frequently lines up with something outside that wall. I have seen deterioration concentrated along a single side of a building that corresponded closely to a run of clogged gutters and buried downspout lines that no longer drained.

## Establishing the extent

The extent of decay is usually larger than what is visible. Decay often progresses inside a member while the surface remains intact, and paint or finishes can hide it entirely. The field work is about finding the boundary between sound and damaged wood, member by member.

I probe the framing with an awl or a similar pointed tool, which penetrates decayed wood easily and sound wood with difficulty. I sound members with a hammer, since decayed wood produces a dull sound. I take readings with a moisture meter, which shows which areas are wet now and helps connect the damage to its source. Where finishes cover the framing in the area of concern, some of them have to be removed before the framing can be evaluated, and I tell the owner that before the visit so the site is ready.

The result is a record of which members are damaged, how far the damage extends along each one, and which members are sound. That record is what a contractor needs to price the repair and what the repair design is based on.

## Insects and decay

Owners often ask whether the damage was caused by termites or by water. The two look different when the wood is opened. Termite damage tends to follow the grain in hollowed galleries, often with soil-like material inside and with mud tubes on the foundation. Decay produces wood that is soft, cracked across the grain into small blocks, or stringy and fibrous, depending on the type of fungus. Carpenter ants, which are common in Ohio and tunnel into wet wood, leave clean, smooth galleries and push sawdust out of the wood. More than one cause can be present in the same member, and old damage of either kind can be hard to distinguish.

The structural evaluation is concerned with what capacity remains, whatever the cause. Whether insects are active, and whether treatment is needed, is a question for a licensed pest control professional, and a structural report does not substitute for a pest inspection. Where the evidence is ambiguous, I say so in the report.

## Find the water before designing the repair

Replacing deteriorated framing without stopping the water that caused it allows the same damage to develop again. The repair recommendations therefore start with the source: gutters and downspouts, grading, failed flashing, leaking plumbing, condensation, and crawlspace conditions that keep the framing damp. A repair report that addresses only the wood is incomplete.

## Repair approaches

Most repairs fall into a few general types.

**Sistering** places a new member alongside a damaged one and fastens it to the sound length of the original. For a decayed joist end, the new member bears on the support itself, since fastening it only to the sound part of the joist does not deliver load to the support.

**Replacement with temporary support** removes a damaged section, such as a length of sill plate or rim joist, while the structure above is shored, and replaces it with new material. Wood in contact with concrete or masonry is replaced with preservative-treated lumber, and so is framing close to exposed soil in a crawlspace. Fasteners and hangers in treated lumber need corrosion protection, typically hot-dip galvanizing or stainless steel.

**New bearing** adds a beam, a post, or a footing where the original support has deteriorated beyond repair or where the original arrangement was inadequate. The posts and footings for a new beam follow the same principles described in the article [Removing a Wall](/articles/removing-a-wall/).

The repair report identifies which members are replaced, which are reinforced, and which remain. Because opening finishes often reveals more damage, the report also states which decisions about newly exposed members are left to the contractor's judgment and on what basis, so the work can continue without a return visit for each member.

## When the city is involved

Some cities require a structural engineer's evaluation before they issue a permit to repair damaged bearing framing, particularly in rental property and in buildings being rehabilitated after a period of vacancy. In that case the report is written for the building official as much as for the owner. It states what was observed, what is required, and what the repair must accomplish, clearly enough that the official can check the completed work against it.
