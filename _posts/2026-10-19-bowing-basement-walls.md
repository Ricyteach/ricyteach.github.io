Older houses and small apartment buildings in the Dayton and Columbus areas, where most of my site visits are, often have basement walls built of hollow concrete block. When one of those walls begins to lean or bow inward, a home inspector notices it, a buyer or an agent reads the word "bowing" in the report, and somebody calls a structural engineer, often with a purchase deadline approaching. This article explains what is happening in that wall and what the realistic options are.

## What the wall is doing

A basement wall is a vertical slab with soil pressing against one face. It is supported at the top by the floor framing that bears on it and at the bottom by the footing and the floor slab, and the soil pressure bends it between those supports the way a floor joist bends under the weight placed on it.

A hollow block wall resists that bending poorly. The mortar joints have very little tensile strength, and many older walls have little or no reinforcing steel in the block cells. When the bending exceeds what the wall can resist, it cracks on the inside face, where the tension is greatest, and the wall begins to move inward at that crack.

## Reading the cracks

A horizontal crack along a mortar joint, somewhere in the middle portion of the wall height, is the typical sign of bending under soil pressure. The wall is hinging at that joint.

Stair-step cracks running diagonally through the mortar toward the corners often accompany the horizontal crack. The corners are stiff, because the intersecting wall braces them, so the middle of a long wall moves while its ends cannot, and the masonry between them cracks to accommodate the difference.

Displacement at the bottom of the wall, where the lowest course has slid inward on the footing or past the edge of the slab, is a different behavior. The wall is sliding at its base instead of bending between its supports, and a repair has to address that.

Vertical cracks, and cracks wider at the top than at the bottom, usually point to settlement instead of soil pressure, and a bowing wall can have both kinds.

## Measuring instead of estimating

How far a wall has moved is a measurement, and I record it as one. I set a string line or a long level against the wall and measure the inward displacement at several heights and at several points along the length, and I measure the width of the horizontal crack at several locations. Those numbers allow the wall to be compared with itself later.

I also examine what sits on top of the wall: whether the floor framing still bears fully on the sill plate, whether the sill plate is anchored to the wall, and whether the top of the wall has pulled away from the framing. A wall that has separated from the floor framing at the top has lost one of its two supports, and that changes the analysis entirely.

## Why the soil pushes harder now

The wall was presumably adequate for many decades, so the useful question is what changed. The usual answer is water. Saturated soil pushes harder than dry soil, and clay soil that takes on water can swell and push harder still. Water reaches the soil behind the wall from clogged gutters, downspouts that discharge at the foundation, buried downspout lines that have broken or filled with debris, and grading that slopes toward the house.

Frost is the second cause, since wet soil that freezes against the upper part of the wall expands. The third is added load at the surface near the wall, such as a driveway or a heavy structure built close to the foundation.

## Drainage comes first

Whatever structural repair is selected, it does not address the cause if water continues to collect behind the wall. The first recommendation in almost every report I write on a bowing wall concerns gutters, downspouts, and grading. That work costs little compared with a structural repair, and it reduces the pressure the repaired wall has to resist.

## Repair options in general terms

Repairs fall into a few families. The right one depends on how far the wall has moved, whether it is still moving, whether it is bending or sliding, and the access available.

Steel bracing against the inside face spans from the basement floor to the floor framing above. It requires sound connections at both ends, and the floor framing has to be checked for the force the brace delivers to it.

Wall anchors pass through the wall to plates buried in the yard and resist the soil pressure through tension in the rod. They need room outside the wall and soil capable of resisting the plate.

Carbon fiber straps bonded to the inside face add tensile strength to the masonry. They suit walls that have moved only slightly, and they do not straighten a wall.

Excavating the outside, straightening or rebuilding the wall, and installing drainage while the soil is removed is the most thorough option and the most disruptive. It becomes the practical answer when the wall has moved too far to be braced in its current position.

I do not recommend a particular product or installer. I describe what the repair must accomplish, and where the work needs a designed detail for a permit or for a contractor to build from, I provide one.

## The question an inspection report cannot answer

A home inspection establishes that a wall is bowed. It cannot establish whether the wall is still moving, and that is usually the question that decides the repair.

Evidence on site helps: weathered or sharp crack edges, patching that has cracked again, and debris collected inside the crack. When the evidence is not conclusive, monitoring answers the question. Crack gauges or marked measurement points, read through a wet season, show whether the movement is continuing.
