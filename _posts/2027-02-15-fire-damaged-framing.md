After a fire in a house or a small apartment building, the owner, the insurer, or the investor who has bought the building to rehabilitate it eventually has to decide which framing stays and which comes out. When the building is under a city order, the city often requires a structural engineer to evaluate the framing before it issues permits for the repair. This article explains what happens to wood in a fire, how the evaluation is done, and where the structural question ends. It is written for owners, investors, and contractors in the Dayton, Springfield, and Columbus areas, where I make site visits.

## How wood behaves in a fire

Wood burns from the outside in. As the surface burns, it turns to char, a black layer of carbon with a cracked pattern on its face. Char has essentially no strength, but it insulates the wood behind it and slows the burning. A large wood member therefore loses its section gradually, at a fairly steady rate, from each face exposed to the fire.

Behind the char there is a thin zone of wood that was heated without burning. Its strength and stiffness were reduced while it was hot, and some of that reduction remains after it cools.

A heavy beam or a thick joist that looks badly burned on its surface may retain most of its original section once the char is removed, because charring advances slowly into a large section. A member that looks sound may still be weakened, because it was heated without charring, because the char is on a face that cannot be seen, or because it was cut or burned at a notch or a hole. Small members, such as two-by-four studs and engineered joists with thin webs, lose a larger share of their section to the same depth of char and can fail quickly. The evaluation begins with appearance and is decided by measurement.

## How the evaluation proceeds

**Removing finishes.** Plaster, drywall, paneling, and ceilings in and near the fire area have to come off before the framing can be evaluated. Fire travels through wall cavities, floor cavities, and attics, so damage often extends past the room where the fire started. I tell the owner before the visit which areas need to be opened.

**Measuring the residual section.** For each affected member, the char is scraped or probed away to sound wood, and the remaining width and depth are measured. The remaining section, with an allowance for the heated zone, is what I evaluate against the loads the member supports. A member that lost only a small amount of section and still meets the requirements can remain. Some members can be reinforced. Others have to be replaced.

**Checking connections and fasteners.** Nails, bolts, joist hangers, truss plates, and other steel hardware conduct heat into the wood around them. The wood around a fastener can char inside the member even when its face looks only discolored, and that wood is what grips the fastener. Joist hangers and truss plates are thin, heat quickly, and can distort, and the teeth of a truss plate grip only a shallow depth of wood. Trusses and engineered joists depend on their connections and their thin parts, so I examine them closely, and replacement is often required.

**Checking for water damage.** Firefighting puts a large amount of water into a building, and a vacant building after a fire is often left open to rain and snow for months. Wet framing, wet subfloor, and wet plaster add weight and can lead to decay if they do not dry out. The decay, the soft sill plates, and the damaged joist ends that follow are the conditions described in the article [Rot, Insects, and Water: Evaluating Damaged Wood Framing](/articles/damaged-wood-framing/), and they are part of the same evaluation.

**Looking beyond the wood.** Heat can crack or spall concrete and masonry, deteriorate mortar in a chimney or a foundation wall, and distort steel beams and columns. Where the fire reached any of these, they are evaluated as well.

## What the structural evaluation covers

A structural evaluation addresses the capacity of the framing and the foundation: which members can remain, which need reinforcement, which have to be replaced, and what the repair must accomplish. The deliverable is a stamped letter report that identifies the members and the repairs clearly enough for a contractor to price the work and for the building official to check it.

Fire separation, egress, and the other life safety provisions of the building code are architectural questions. Fire separation means the fire-resistance-rated walls and floor and ceiling assemblies that separate dwelling units from each other and from a garage. Egress means the exits, stairs, and openings people use to leave the building. Whether the rehabilitated building meets the building code in those respects is a question for an architect and for the building department. When a city order requires architectural plans for fire separation, I refer that part of the work to an architect. My letter states which items of the order it addresses.

## Repair approaches

Most repairs fall into a few general types. **Cleaning and leaving in place** applies to members with surface char only, where the measured section is adequate. The char is removed, and the wood is often coated to control odor. **Sistering** fastens a new member alongside a damaged one, and in most designs the new member reaches its own bearing at each end. **Replacement** removes a damaged member while the structure above is shored and installs a new one. **Replacing trusses or engineered joists** usually follows when their connections or thin parts were heated.

When a city is involved, the letter written for the building official follows the approach described in the article [When a Report Says to Call a Structural Engineer](/articles/call-a-structural-engineer/).

## What to send me

Send photographs of the fire area and the rooms next to it, with wide views and close views of any exposed framing. Include the city order or notice if there is one, the age and number of units of the building, any insurance adjuster's report, and what has already been removed or repaired.
