On a flat commercial roof, a solar array is often held down by weight. The racking sits on the roof membrane, and concrete blocks placed in trays on the racking keep it from lifting or sliding in wind. Ballasted arrays avoid roof penetrations, or limit them to a few mechanical attachments, which roof owners usually prefer.

An attached residential array is the subject of the article [Solar Panels on an Existing Roof: What the Structural Letter Checks](/articles/rooftop-solar-letter/). That array relies on the capacity of each screw in the framing. A ballasted array relies on its own weight, and weight is a load as well as a resistance.

## A minimum dead load problem

For most structures, more dead load means more demand. For a ballasted array, the dead load of the racking and the ballast is what keeps the array in place, so the design question is whether there is enough weight under the least favorable assumptions.

The load combinations in ASCE 7, the loading standard published by the American Society of Civil Engineers, include a combination for exactly this case, with the dead load counted at a reduced value against the design wind load. The article [The Load Combination Is the Design](/articles/load-combinations/) describes why dead load needs a minimum treatment as well as a maximum one. For a ballasted array, the minimum treatment decides the amount of ballast, and the maximum treatment, with the full ballast weight and snow, decides what the roof has to support. The edition of ASCE 7 that applies is the one adopted by the jurisdiction where the building stands.

## Three questions

A ballasted array design answers three separate questions, and each needs its own check.

**Staying in place.** The ballast has to resist uplift, sliding, and overturning of each section of the array under wind. Sliding resistance depends on the friction between the racking feet or pads and the roof surface, which varies with the membrane type, the pad material, and whether the surface is wet. Friction values come from testing for the specific combination of pad and membrane. In seismic regions, the loading standard permits an unattached array only under stated conditions, including interconnection of the array and clearance for the distance it may slide during an earthquake. An array that does not meet them needs mechanical attachment.

**Supporting the ballast.** Ballast is heavy and it is concentrated. The weight sits in rows at the racking supports, and the corners and edges of the array receive more ballast than the middle. The roof deck, the joists, the beams or joist girders, and their connections all have to be checked for the added weight and its distribution, along with the snow that the array causes to collect. Many existing flat roofs have modest reserve beyond their original design loads, and the array layout sometimes has to be arranged around the capacity of particular members.

**Protecting the roof assembly.** The roofing membrane and the insulation below it receive the concentrated pressure of the racking feet. Rigid insulation compresses under sustained load, and a membrane can be damaged by abrasion as the array moves slightly with thermal expansion and wind. The racking manufacturer and the roofing manufacturer both have requirements for protection pads, maximum bearing pressure, and drainage paths. Where the roof is under warranty, the design should confirm these requirements with the roofing manufacturer.

## Wind tunnel data and code pressure coefficients

A low tilted array on a flat roof is a complicated flow problem. The panels shelter each other, the gaps between rows and under the panels change the pressure, and the array position relative to the roof edge and to parapets changes it further. Since the 2016 edition, ASCE 7 has included pressure coefficients for tilted arrays on low slope roofs within stated limits on tilt, panel size, and row spacing. Many racking manufacturers also commission wind tunnel testing of their specific product, and the results are published as pressure or ballast data for different positions on the roof.

Wind tunnel data describes the tested product under the tested conditions. Applying it to a specific site means confirming that the site falls within those conditions: the building height and roof dimensions, the parapet height, the array tilt, the row spacing, and the gap at the roof edge. It also means combining the tested coefficients with the wind speed, exposure, and other site factors from the code in the way the test report specifies, and confirming that the testing and its application meet the requirements of the edition of ASCE 7 in force. When a site falls outside the tested range, the manufacturer data does not apply directly, and the design needs another basis. I apply wind tunnel data to specific sites and check it against the ASCE 7 pressure coefficients as part of this work.

## Roof zones and the ballast layout

Wind pressure on a flat roof is highest near the corners and edges, where the flow separates from the roof, and lower across the middle of the roof. ASCE 7 and the wind tunnel data both divide the roof into zones for this reason, and the ballast required in a corner zone is substantially more than the ballast required in the interior.

A single ballast amount used across the whole array is wrong in one direction or the other. If it is set for the interior, the corner and edge sections are under-ballasted and may move in a design windstorm. If it is set for the corners, the interior sections have weight they do not need, and that excess weight is load the roof structure has to support for the life of the array. The practical result is a ballast layout drawing that shows the number of blocks at each support, zone by zone, and an installation that follows it.
