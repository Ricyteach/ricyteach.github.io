A marble placed on the floor rolls to one side of the room. A door that once closed now swings open on its own, or its top corner rubs the frame. A buyer feels the floor dip near the middle of a room. Sloping and sagging floors are common in older houses in the Dayton, Springfield, and Columbus areas, and the cause is usually somewhere other than the floor where the slope is noticed.

## Measure the slope

A slope felt underfoot is real, but the sensation does not give its size or shape, so I measure it. A long level with a ruler, a digital level, or a laser level set up in the middle of the room gives the elevation of the floor at many points, and those readings show the shape of the floor.

The number and spacing of points decide what the measurements can show. Readings along one wall cannot distinguish a floor that sags in the middle from a house that tilts as a whole, and readings across each room on every level can. The patterns differ:

- A dip that is lowest near the middle of a span and rises toward the supports at each end is a sag, which points to the joists or the beam under that area.
- A floor that drops steadily from one side of the house to the other, on every level, points to the foundation under the low side.
- A floor that is low along one line through the house, with the low line repeated on the floor above, points to a beam or a line of posts under that line.

I also measure the joists and beams that are visible from the basement or crawlspace: their size, spacing, span, and bearing, and any sag in the beam itself. Following the measured low area down to the framing and foundation below it is the method described in the article [Reading an Existing Structure Before You Analyze It](/articles/condition-assessment/).

## The common causes

**Undersized or overspanned joists.** Many older houses were framed with joists that would not meet a current span table, and some were built with joists spanning farther than the lumber could support without excessive deflection. They have usually been sagging for many decades.

**Joists notched or cut for plumbing.** A drain line, a heating duct, or a vent often runs across the joists, and an installer who cuts a deep notch in the bottom edge of a joist, or cuts through it entirely, removes much of its strength.

**A removed or relocated post.** Basement posts are sometimes removed or moved to open the space. The beam above then spans farther than it was built to span, and it sags.

**A beam with a failed splice.** Built-up wood beams in older houses are made of several boards nailed together, with the joints between boards staggered. If the joints line up, or fall in the middle of a span between posts, the beam is weak at that point and can bend sharply there.

**Settlement of an interior pier.** Interior posts often stand on a small footing, a stack of masonry, or simply the basement floor slab. If that support settles, the post drops and the floor above it drops with it. Damp soil, a footing that was never built, and a slab that was never designed for the load are common reasons.

**Long-term creep.** Wood under constant load continues to deflect slowly for years, and more so when it is damp. Old floors that sag evenly, with framing that otherwise meets its loads, have often crept.

Damaged wood is a further cause. Decay or insect damage at a sill plate or joist end allows the floor to drop at its edge.

## Why jacking back to level is slow, and sometimes the wrong goal

When a beam or joists are raised with jacks, everything above them moves: walls, door frames, plaster, tile, plumbing, and the upper floors. Over decades those parts have adjusted to the sloped position. Lifting quickly cracks plaster and tile and can damage plumbing, so lifting is done in small increments over days or weeks, with the structure checked at each step.

Raising a floor all the way to level is often unnecessary. Wood that has crept for decades will not straighten fully, and forcing it can split it. A floor that is held where it is with a firm support, with some of the sag removed, is frequently the better result. Before any lifting begins, the support beneath the jack has to be adequate, and the cause of the slope has to be corrected, or the floor will settle again.

## Repair the cause first

Most repairs fall into a few general types, and the choice follows from the cause.

**Sistering** fastens a new joist alongside an undersized, notched, or damaged joist, over its full length where possible, and in most designs the new member reaches its own bearing at each end.

**A new beam and posts with footings** shortens the span of overspanned joists or replaces a beam that cannot be repaired. The posts need footings, which often do not exist yet, so the work frequently includes cutting the basement slab and placing a new footing under each post. The same considerations apply when a bearing wall is replaced with a beam, as the article [Removing a Wall](/articles/removing-a-wall/) describes.

**Foundation repair** addresses a settled pier or a settled exterior wall, and where settlement is the suspected cause, a geotechnical investigation may be the next step.

**Restoring a removed post or repairing a splice** returns the beam to the support arrangement it was built for.

Adding a jack post under a sagging floor leaves the cause in place. Unless the post bears on an adequate footing, and the cause of the sag is understood, the floor continues to move. An adjustable steel jack post left as a permanent support also has to be a type the building department accepts for that use.
